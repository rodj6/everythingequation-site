Finite-lattice positivity

Reflection positivity starts at the finite Wilson law. Cutting a torus into two reflected halves rewrites each seam contribution as a positive kernel, so that a future observable paired with its reflected conjugate has nonnegative expectation. The finite-dimensional statement gives the null-space quotient used later to build a Hilbert space. It is also the input to the chessboard bounds: positivity converts a product of separated chamber observables into a product of individual rooted bounds. The details below retain the seam and orientation conventions needed for both uses.

A site gauge change multiplies a link on its two ends; by Haar invariance its change of variables preserves the product prior. Its plaquette holonomy is conjugated at the base site, so the trace and the action are unchanged. The normalized law is consequently gauge invariant. A signed lattice permutation merely reindexes plaquettes, possibly reversing their holonomies; inversion preserves their real traces. It too preserves both the prior and the law. Reflection positivity is the nonnegativity of the expectation of a future observable times its reflected complex conjugate. It is a property of this finite law, before any quotient or completion. The Wilson measures used throughout have that property at every nonnegative coupling. We give the finite-dimensional argument, since it also supplies the rooted bounds used in extraction.

For a finite alphabet of functions, choose a cell assignment maximizing . Both folded values in (RP4) are at most this maximum. If the maximum is positive, (RP4) forces both folded values to equal it; if it is zero this is immediate. Hence the property of being a maximizing assignment is preserved by every fold. Such a fold-closed property has a constant assignment: in one coordinate start with a one-cell-wide strip of identical slices and fold at its preceding boundary. This extends the strip by one slice while preserving the property. Continue until a half-period is filled, then reflect it to fill that coordinate. A fold in another coordinate preserves equality along the coordinates already filled. Repeat in all four coordinates. This is a finite construction and needs no power-of-two restriction on the number of slices.
