4.1 The integer bound and the case-I count

The following argument isolates the precise arithmetic assertion needed from the unitary rank-three con-struction. It is useful to do so before discussing its publication inputs: the integer produced there must be independent of the coefficient prime. A separate integer for each prime would not give the estimate below.

Write v_{ℓ}(e, ψ) ≥0 for the normalized finite length denoted Vn in the source, and write λ_{ℓ}(e, ψ) for its length before conversion to a real number. The assertion λ_{ℓ}(e, ψ) < ∞ is retained separately, so no value assigned to an infinite length enters an inequality. For G_{N} = (ℤ/Nℤ)^{×} and H ≤G_{N}, put

𝒪_{ℓ}(H) = {ψ : G_{N} −→ℚ_{ℓ} : ker ψ = H},

with values restricted to the coefficient ring in use when necessary. These sets are finite. A nonempty such set has cyclic quotient G_{N}/H, because a finite subgroup of a field’s multiplicative group is cyclic.

Input 4.2 (The common integer congruence bound). For each number field K, there are natural numbers c_{5}, A with the following property. For e ≥1, N > 0, H ≤G_{N}, and a prime r ∤30N| disc K|, there is an integer b_{r} ̸= 0 such that

log |b_{r}| ≤c_{5}φ([G_{N} : H]) N(e + 1))^{A} log r. (76)

If ℓ̸= r and the orbit 𝒪_{ℓ}(H) contains an admissible character, then every character in that orbit has finite length and ∑

v_{ℓ}(e, ψ) ≤2 ord_{ℓ}(b_{r}). (77)

The integer b_{r} works for every coefficient prime and coefficient ring simultaneously.

Here admissibility is the source’s explicit predicate GD. Its conditions are: ℓ is odd, e ≥1, N is even, ℓ∤ ord ψ, the part of ψ on the ℓ-primary component of G_{N} has square one, and ψ(−1) = (−1)^{e}; K is imaginary quadratic, ℓ∤| disc K|h_{K}, gcd(N, | disc K|) = 1, and ℓ−1 ∤2e. There is also the required independence condition on the quadratic character of K: the intersection of the kernels of the mod-ℓ cyclotomic character and the prime-to-ℓ, off-ℓ part ψ^{(ℓ)} of ψ is not contained in G_{K} ⊂G_{ℚ}. In the source, ψ^{(ℓ)} is chiP: first remove the ℓ-power-order part of ψ, then divide by its restriction to the ℓ-primary component of G_{N}. The twists and the kernel condition are part of the input; an estimate for arbitrary characters is not being assumed.

Lemma 4.3 (Two auxiliary primes remove their exceptions). Assume Input 4.2. Let r ̸= r^{′} be primes prime to 30N| disc K|, and let L be any finite set of coefficient primes for which 𝒪_{ℓ}(H) contains an admissible character. Then all its orbit lengths are finite and

log ℓ v_{ℓ}(e, ψ) ≤2c_{5}φ([G_{N} : H]) N(e + 1))^{A}(log r + log r^{′}). (78)

Proof. If L is empty the assertions are immediate. Otherwise its admissible character gives e ≥1, so obtain the two nonzero integers b_{r}, b_{r}′ from the input. For ℓ̸= r use b_{r}; for ℓ= r use b_{r}′, which is allowed because r ̸= r^{′}. In either case all lengths are finite and the orbit sum is at most 2 ord_{ℓ}(b_{r}) + 2 ord_{ℓ}(b_{r}′).

For every nonzero integer b the pairwise coprime prime powers ℓ^{ordℓb}, ℓ∈L, have product dividing |b|. Their product is positive, so taking logarithms gives

log ℓ ord_{ℓ}b ≤ log |b|.

Multiply the preceding orbit inequalities by log ℓ≥0, sum them, and apply this divisor inequality to the two integers. The result is at most 2 log |b_{r}| + 2 log |b_{r}′|. Substitution of (76) is exactly (78).

Lemma 4.4 (Counting the distinct kernels). If G is a finite abelian group and T is a finite set of subgroups with cyclic quotients, then ∑

Proof. For a cyclic quotient G/H of order m, its faithful complex characters are specified by the primitive mth roots assigned to a generator. There are φ(m) of them. Composing with the quotient map gives distinct characters of G whose kernel is exactly H. Characters obtained from different H have different kernels, so the resulting sets are disjoint. They lie in the character group of G. To count that group, write G as a product of finite cyclic groups: a character is specified independently by a root of unity of the appropriate order on each cyclic generator. Their number is the product of the cyclic orders, namely |G|. Counting the disjoint subsets proves the inequality.
