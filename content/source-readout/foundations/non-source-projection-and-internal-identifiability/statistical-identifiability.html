Statistical identifiability

The preceding sections treated probes as deterministic maps, and the ceiling obtained there is correspondingly deterministic. Real experiments return random data, and the ceiling must therefore be established again, from its own hypothesis, at the level of probability laws. We stress that this is not a restatement of Theorem 7.2 but an independent result resting on the separate assumption (30): only at the level of complete outcome laws does one earn the stronger conclusion that no test, at any sample size and with any randomization, can discriminate.

Remark 47 (The three distinctions that must not collapse).

Theorem 8.2 is easy to over-read in both directions.

Forward. (S1)–(S3) say that when the complete outcome laws are exactly equal, statistical discrimination is not difficult but impossible: not by any statistic, not by any test, not at any sample size, not with any amount of randomization, not asymptotically. There is no procedure to improve, because the two hypotheses induce literally the same distribution over all possible data. This is the strongest negative available, and it is what Corollary 8.4 exploits.

Backward. (S4) says only that unequal laws differ on some event. It emphatically does not say that a single observation classifies the state with certainty. If μs0(A)=0.51\mu_{s_0}(A)=0.51μs0​​(A)=0.51 and μs1(A)=0.49\mu_{s_1}(A)=0.49μs1​​(A)=0.49, the laws are unequal, ∥μs0−μs1∥TV≥0.02>0\|\mu_{s_0}-\mu_{s_1}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\ge0.02>0∥μs0​​−μs1​​∥TV​≥0.02>0, and yet one observation of 1A\mathbf 1_A1A​ is nearly worthless. What (S4) supplies is a nonzero signal, hence , given i.i.d. repetition, which is an additional assumption about D\mathcal DD and μs\mu_sμs​ , consistency of a suitable test as the sample size grows. One must therefore keep apart: exact identifiability (distinct states have distinct laws); asymptotic identifiability (some consistent procedure separates them in the limit); and finite-sample power (how much data a given error level costs). Only the first is a purely measure-theoretic property, and only the failure of the first is what §7 and Corollary 8.4 are about.

Remark 49 (The two ceilings, stated separately).

Corollary 8.4 is the probabilistic analogue of Theorem 7.2, and (30) the probabilistic analogue of Assumption 7.1. It is not a consequence of them. The two results must be kept apart, because their hypotheses differ and so do their conclusions.

Deterministic ceiling. Hypothesis: every admissible probe factors through the readout, Pi=Pˉi∘pP_i=\bar P_i\circ pPi​=Pˉi​∘p. Conclusion: within a readout fiber, all probe values coincide, so no deterministic selector, statistic, or post-processing rule applied to those values can distinguish its members (Theorem 7.2, Corollary 7.3).

Statistical ceiling. Hypothesis: the complete outcome law factors through the readout, μs=Mp(s)\mu_s=\mathsf M_{p(s)}μs​=Mp(s)​. Conclusion: within a readout fiber, the complete data laws are equal, so no statistic, no randomized test, no sample size, and no asymptotic procedure can discriminate; the total-variation distance is zero (Corollary 8.4).

The second does not follow from the first. Deterministic factorization constrains probe values; it says nothing about the law of a stochastic experiment unless that experiment’s complete law is itself identified as a probe factoring through ppp , an identification which must be made explicitly, and which is exactly the content of (30). Both are hypotheses about the model, to be discharged by the model. Neither is a theorem about observers in general.

The exact relations among the three obstructions

We can now state precisely how kinematic incompleteness, dynamical nonclosure, and statistical nonidentifiability are related. They are not logically independent, and it is worth being exact, since the pattern of implication is what licenses , and what forbids , inferring one from another.

Proposition 8.6 (Logical relations).

Let p ⁣:S→T1p\colon S\to T_1p:S→T1​ be an exact readout.

  1. Dynamical nonclosure implies noninjectivity. If Φt\Phi_tΦt​ is fiber-breaking (Case II of Theorem 5.2), then ppp is noninjective. Thus kinematic incompleteness is necessary for fiber-breaking.

  2. The converse fails. Noninjectivity of ppp does not imply fiber-breaking: there exist noninjective ppp with fiber-preserving, autonomous dynamics. Hence kinematic incompleteness is not sufficient for dynamical nonclosure, and, equivalently, autonomy of the projected law does not imply injectivity of ppp.

  3. Statistical nonidentifiability is unconditionally unrelated to ppp. Without a hypothesis tying {μs}\{\mu_s\}{μs​} to ppp, the existence of s0≠s1s_0\neq s_1s0​=s1​ with μs0=μs1\mu_{s_0}=\mu_{s_1}μs0​​=μs1​​ neither implies nor is implied by noninjectivity of ppp.
